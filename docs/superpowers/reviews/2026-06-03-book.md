## BLOCKING Findings

### [B1] Chess-positions figure contradicts itself between Chapter 6 and Chapter 7

- **Location:** `chapters/06_reinforcement_learning.tex:190` and `chapters/07_generalization.tex:17`
- **Finding:** Chapter 6 states "Chess has about $10^{120}$ positions." Chapter 7, one chapter later, states "Chess has about $10^{44}$ legal positions." These are two different quantities (Chapter 6's $10^{120}$ approximates the Shannon number, i.e., the number of possible game trees/sequences; Chapter 7's $10^{44}$ approximates the number of distinct legal board positions). Both appear in nearly identical context ("tabular Q-learning does not scale"). A reader arriving at Chapter 7 will notice the order-of-magnitude contradiction and lose confidence. The correct values are approximately $10^{44}$ legal positions and $10^{120}$ game-tree nodes. The chapters should either use the same quantity (with the same label) or be explicit that they are measuring different things.
- **Auditors:** math-auditor, quality-auditor, cross-ref-auditor
- **Suggested fix:** Standardize to one consistent number and one consistent concept across both chapters. The cleanest fix: in Ch 6 use $10^{44}$ legal positions (matching Ch 7 and the established literature on chess complexity), or in both chapters note the distinction between legal positions ($\approx 10^{44}$) and game-tree nodes ($\approx 10^{120}$). The Shannon number $10^{120}$ is the more widely cited figure in popular writing about chess AI; the simpler fix may be to bring Ch 7 into alignment with Ch 6 by switching to "game positions/nodes" and citing $10^{120}$, with a brief parenthetical noting the alternative count.

### [B2] The Solomonoff convergence bound (Ch 4) is misstated relative to the authoritative math-grounding doc

- **Location:** `chapters/04_solomonoff_induction.tex:165–199` (dominance theorem section)
- **Finding:** The chapter's dominance-theorem discussion asserts the property as: $M(x) \geq c_\mu \cdot \mu(x)$ for all $x$, with convergence of predictions to the true distribution. This is correctly stated. However, the convergence statement ("the predictions of $M$ converge to the predictions of $\mu$ as data accumulates") is left entirely informal without any pointer to the KL-divergence bound given in math-grounding.md (§2: Solomonoff Convergence), which states the bound $\sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{E}_\mu[\mathrm{KL}(\mu(\cdot|x_{<t}) \| M(\cdot|x_{<t}))] \leq K(\mu) \ln 2$. Given the book's stated commitment ("the mathematics must be real; no hand-waving dressed up as a theorem"), presenting a dominance theorem with an informal convergence claim and no pointer to the actual bound qualifies as hand-waving dressed up as a result. This is the single most technical claim in Part I and the most important place for a footnote or brief formal statement.
- **Auditors:** spec-auditor, math-auditor
- **Suggested fix:** Add a footnote at the end of the dominance-theorem section citing the KL-divergence convergence bound by name, citing Hutter (2005), and giving the inequality $\sum_{t} \mathbb{E}_\mu[\mathrm{KL}] \leq K(\mu) \ln 2$. The main text can remain informal; the footnote carries the formal anchor.

### [B3] Inner alignment chapter (Ch 10) treats deceptive alignment as straightforward structural necessity; omits the key caveat that it requires situational awareness as a prerequisite, which is not established until Ch 15

- **Location:** `chapters/10_inner_alignment.tex:97–141` (deceptive alignment section)
- **Finding:** The chapter argues that a misaligned mesa-optimizer "should expect to do better, by its own lights, if it pretends to be aligned during training." This argument is presented as following from optimization alone. However, the chapter's own premise list (lines 99–103) correctly specifies that the mesa-optimizer must have situational awareness and planning capacity, but then the chapter immediately drops this into the structural argument as though it were guaranteed. Situational awareness is, in 2026, a current empirical finding (presented in Ch 15 as already-present but gradient-form), not a necessary consequence of optimization. The chapter in the clean-theory Part III is treating a contingent empirical precondition as a necessary structural feature. This is the kind of conflation that the lore explicitly warns against ("the mathematics must be real"). The argument remains valid as a conditional ("if a system has situational awareness and planning capacity, then..."), but the chapter needs to be clearer that the antecedent is not itself structurally guaranteed.
- **Auditors:** spec-auditor, quality-auditor
- **Suggested fix:** Add a brief hedge in the deceptive alignment section: the argument is valid conditioned on the three premises, and the premises are individually contingent. The empirical evidence that situational awareness exists in current models lives in Ch 15 and should be cross-referenced here. A sentence such as "Whether this third condition is met in current systems is the empirical question Chapter 15 addresses" would close the gap.

### [B4] AIXI action-selection formula in Ch 8 uses $2^{-K(\nu)}$ as the prior weight but the lore (math-grounding.md §5) gives the prior over environments as a semimeasure prior, and the formula as stated omits the normalization / definition of $V^*$ as a recursive object in a way that could mislead

- **Location:** `chapters/08_aixi.tex:41–48`
- **Finding:** The AIXI action formula is:
  $$a_{t+1} = \arg\max_a \sum_{o,r}\sum_\nu 2^{-K(\nu)} \cdot \nu(o,r \mid h_t a) \cdot [r + \gamma \cdot V^*(h_t a o r)]$$
  The math is consistent with Hutter (2005). However, the prose immediately following says "Each symbol is unpacked below; $V^*$ in particular is defined two paragraphs on, so read past it for now." The reader is asked to accept a formula with an undefined recursive term ($V^*$) before its definition. This is a pedagogical problem that the book's own stated pedagogy ("conceptual explanation first, then formalization") is supposed to avoid. Additionally, $V^*$ is described two paragraphs later as "the optimal expected discounted return from a given history," which is correct but does not make explicit that $V^*$ is itself defined by unrolling the same AIXI action-selection equation recursively — making the whole formula self-referential (a fixed-point equation). For a lay reader, this self-reference is the hardest part of the formula, and it is the part the prose does not develop. The math-grounding doc notes "unrolled, it considers every future trajectory each $\nu$ admits" but the chapter prose does not make this recursion explicit.
- **Auditors:** math-auditor, quality-auditor
- **Suggested fix:** Either (a) introduce a simplified two-step version of the formula before the general recursive one, making the self-reference explicit, or (b) restructure the paragraph order: define $V^*$ qualitatively first, then present the full formula. A footnote clarifying that $V^*$ is itself the value of acting optimally under the same posterior (i.e., the fixed-point structure) would suffice as a minimal fix.

### [S2] Chapter 5 (The Agent): the umbrella worked example contains a subtle modeling asymmetry that may confuse careful readers

- **Location:** `chapters/05_the_agent.tex:144–173`
- **Finding:** The umbrella example states:
  - "Dry with an umbrella (annoying to carry, but dry either way): $u = -1$."
  Then computes: $EU(\text{take}) = 0.3 \cdot (-1) + 0.7 \cdot (-1) = -1$. This collapses both rain and no-rain outcomes for taking the umbrella to $u = -1$. But the utility specification gives only one value for "Dry with an umbrella" and doesn't separately specify the wet-with-umbrella case (which is physically impossible since the umbrella keeps you dry). The table of utilities is: dry-without ($+2$), dry-with ($-1$), wet ($-5$). There are actually four outcome cells: (take, rain), (take, no rain), (leave, rain), (leave, no rain). The simplification works because the umbrella always prevents wetness, so (take, rain) = (take, no rain) = $-1$. But this implicit assumption (the umbrella always works perfectly) is never stated. A reader who is following carefully may ask "what if the umbrella fails?" The example is fine for its purpose but the hidden assumption needs a brief acknowledgment.
- **Auditors:** quality-auditor
- **Suggested fix:** Add a parenthetical: "(...assuming the umbrella reliably keeps you dry either way, so the rain outcome and the no-rain outcome are the same for that action)."

### [S11] Chapter 8 (AIXI): the claim "Pareto-optimal" is stated without qualification but the Pareto-optimality result requires caveats about the discount factor and the class of environments

- **Location:** `chapters/08_aixi.tex:96–103`
- **Finding:** The chapter states: "**Pareto-optimal.** No other agent dominates AIXI across all computable environments. Any other agent that does better than AIXI in some environment necessarily does worse in another." This is correct in spirit but the statement as given is imprecise. The Pareto-optimality of AIXI (Hutter 2005, Leike and Hutter 2018) holds for self-delimiting environments and under specific conditions on the discount factor. It does not hold in the standard undiscounted return setting, and the Leike-Hutter (2018) paper shows that AIXI is not even weakly Pareto-optimal in some formulations. The math-grounding.md correctly cites "Hutter (2005), Lattimore and Hutter (2014), Leike and Hutter (2018)" but does not spell out the qualifications. For a book that says "the mathematics must be real," an unqualified Pareto-optimality claim is too strong.
- **Auditors:** math-auditor, spec-auditor
- **Suggested fix:** Add a footnote: "Pareto-optimality here is for the discounted setting and for the class of lower-semicomputable environments. Leike and Hutter (2018) show some failure cases in the undiscounted setting. The informal claim that no other agent uniformly dominates AIXI is robust; the formal optimality properties require the discount and the class qualification."
